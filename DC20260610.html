Law forty three of the forty eight laws of power is about the pursuit of genuine influence rather than mere compliance. Robert Greene argues that there are two fundamentally different ways of getting people to do what you want. the first is through force, fear, or material incentive. you can coerce people, you can threaten consequences, or you can offer rewards sufficient to override their preferences. this approach works, to a degree. people do what is required to avoid the stick or obtain the carrot. but it produces a fragile and limited form of influence. the moment your ability to enforce or to reward weakens, the compliance ends. and throughout the period of compliance, you are dealing with people who are at best neutral and at worst actively looking for ways to work around or against you. the second approach is fundamentally different. it aims not at compliance but at genuine commitment. it seeks to win the hearts and minds of the people you need to influence, to make them want to do what you need them to do, to align their desires with your purposes in ways that make coercion unnecessary. this is harder to achieve than simple compliance, but what it produces is incomparably more powerful. a person who is genuinely committed to your goals works with initiative, creativity, and resilience that the merely compliant person never brings. they continue to serve your purposes even when your attention is elsewhere, because they believe in what they are doing, not because they are being watched. Greene begins by establishing the psychological foundation of this law. people are moved primarily by their emotions, their self interest, and their deepest values. the person who can speak to these, who can connect what they are asking with what the other person already wants and cares about, is far more persuasive than the person who simply makes an argument or issues a directive. the argument addresses the rational mind, which is only one of several decision making systems operating in any person. the appeal to emotion and self interest addresses the whole person, and the whole person is what you need to move. the first technique Greene describes is the identification of what each specific person values most. this is not a one size fits all exercise. different people are driven by different things. some are primarily motivated by recognition, by the desire to be seen and appreciated for their contributions. others are driven by security, by the need to know that their position and their future are protected. still others are motivated by a sense of purpose, by the desire to feel that their work is part of something meaningful. and others are driven by specific material interests, by advancement, by resources, or by the accumulation of capabilities. the skilled practitioner of this law learns to read which of these motivations dominates in each specific person and speaks to that motivation directly. the second technique is the connection of your purposes to the other person's existing desires. rather than asking someone to set aside their interests and serve yours, the most effective approach finds the intersection between what you need and what they already want. when you can show someone that doing what you need them to do is also a way of achieving what they most care about, the resistance that would have met a direct request melts away. they are no longer being asked to sacrifice. they are being offered an opportunity. and people respond to opportunities with enthusiasm that they never bring to obligations. Greene also discusses the role of genuine understanding and empathy in this process. the person who takes the time to understand how someone else sees the world, what they are worried about, what they are hoping for, and what they feel has been denied them, is in a far better position to speak to them effectively than the person who approaches every interaction with a pre prepared script. this genuine understanding requires listening more than speaking, asking more than telling, and paying attention to the full human being in front of you rather than just the role they play in your plans. there is also a distinction Greene draws between the short term and long term dimensions of winning hearts and minds. in the short term, a dramatic gesture, a well timed act of generosity, or a compelling appeal to shared values can shift someone's emotional alignment quickly. but lasting influence requires consistency. it requires that every significant interaction with a person reinforces the impression that you understand them, that you value them, and that your purposes genuinely include their interests. a single inconsistency, a moment when your actions reveal that you do not actually care about what you have been claiming to care about, can undo months of carefully built alignment. Greene is also realistic about the limits of this law. not every person can be won over, and not every situation allows for the patient relationship building that genuine alignment requires. in some situations, time pressure or the nature of the opposition makes the slower path of winning hearts and minds impractical. in these cases, the other tools of power come into play. but even in these situations, Greene argues, keeping the long term goal of genuine alignment in view, and taking whatever small steps toward it the situation allows, is always preferable to relying exclusively on the cruder instruments of compliance. in conclusion, law forty three teaches that the deepest and most durable form of power is the kind that operates not through fear or material incentive but through genuine alignment. by working on the hearts and minds of those you need to influence, by connecting your purposes to their values and their interests, and by building relationships based on genuine understanding rather than strategic calculation alone, you create a form of influence that does not require constant enforcement, does not breed resentment, and does not collapse the moment your ability to coerce is weakened. it is harder to build than compliance. but it is worth infinitely more.
