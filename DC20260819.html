the opening chapter of the power of now introduces what Eckhart Tolle presents as the most fundamental and most liberating insight available to any human being, the recognition that you are not your mind. this single statement, which Tolle acknowledges may initially sound strange or even threatening to most people, forms the foundation of everything that follows in the book and represents the entry point into an entirely different relationship with ones own inner life and with the experience of existence itself. the mind, Tolle argues, is a tool, an extraordinary tool with enormous practical value, but a tool that has somehow come to mistake itself for the being that uses it, producing a chronic state of unconscious identification with thought that is the primary source of human psychological suffering. Tolle begins by describing the experience that initiated his own spiritual transformation, a night of profound despair in his late twenties when he found himself so consumed by self hatred and misery that he became aware of something paradoxical, that there seemed to be two of him, the self that was suffering intolerably and the self that was aware of that suffering. this moment of split awareness, in which the observer and the observed became briefly distinguishable, was for Tolle the first glimpse of a dimension of his experience that lay entirely outside the stream of compulsive thought and reactive emotion in which he had previously been completely immersed. what followed, over subsequent hours, was a profound and permanent transformation in his relationship to his own mind. the chapter explains that most people spend their lives entirely identified with what Tolle calls the voice in the head, the largely automatic, compulsive stream of thinking that produces commentary, judgment, fantasy, resentment, anticipation, and anxiety without ceasing from morning to night and often throughout the night as well. this voice is not experienced by most people as a separate phenomenon that can be observed and questioned. it is experienced as oneself, as the most intimate and immediate expression of who one is. and because it is identified with as the self rather than observed from the perspective of a deeper, prior awareness, its contents, its habitual patterns, its chronic commentary and judgment and worry, are experienced as reality rather than as one possible representation of reality among many. Tolle introduces a simple but powerful technique for beginning to dissolve this identification, the practice of watching ones own thinking as an observer without engaging with the content of the thoughts, without arguing with them, suppressing them, or following them further into their habitual chains of association. simply watching, with a quality of interested but non identified attention, the arising and passing of thought itself. Tolle notes that the moment genuine watching begins, something remarkable becomes apparent, that the watcher is not itself a thought. it is a quality of awareness that is prior to and different in nature from the stream of thought it observes. this awareness, Tolle argues, is what you actually are beneath and behind the mental noise that has been mistaken for identity. the chapter introduces the concept of enlightenment within this framework, presenting it not as a supernatural achievement accessible only to specially chosen spiritual individuals but as the natural state that becomes available to anyone who genuinely dis identifies from the thinking mind and begins to inhabit the deeper awareness that thinking has always been obscuring. enlightenment, in Tolles description, is simply the felt sense of being, the direct experience of ones own existence prior to and independent of any mental content, a state of presence and aliveness that is already present as the ground of all experience but that goes unnoticed in the continuous noise of unconscious thinking. the chapter closes by noting that the mind itself is not the enemy and that the goal of the book is not the elimination of thinking but the restoration of thinking to its proper function as a tool in service of a conscious, present awareness rather than as the master that unconsciously drives all of a persons experience and response to life. when thinking is used deliberately in service of the present moment rather than running compulsively as an automatic, identity forming process, it becomes enormously more useful and the quality of life becomes enormously more rich, calm, and fully experienced.
