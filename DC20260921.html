the opening chapters of the one thing introduce the central premise that will organize the entire book, a challenge to the widely accepted belief that extraordinary success requires doing many things well simultaneously. Gary Keller and Jay Papasan argue instead that extraordinary results are directly determined by how few things a person does, not by how many, and that the path to truly remarkable achievement in any domain of life runs through radical simplification rather than through the accumulation of ever more goals, priorities, and simultaneous efforts. the book opening chapter introduces what becomes the central organizing tool of the entire work, a deceptively simple question that the authors call the focusing question, what is the one thing i can do such that by doing it everything else will be easier or unnecessary. this question, the authors argue, cuts through the noise and complexity of modern professional and personal life, forcing a process of ruthless prioritization that most peoples ordinary planning and goal setting processes never actually achieve. rather than generating an ever expanding list of tasks and priorities, the focusing question demands a narrowing process, pushing continually toward the single most consequential action available in any given moment or circumstance. the authors support this argument with an extended and vivid metaphor drawn from the mechanics of falling dominoes, describing research demonstrating that a single domino can knock over another domino approximately fifty percent larger than itself, and that a chain of dominoes, each successively larger than the one before it, can produce a cascade of exponentially increasing force from an initial domino that started remarkably small. the authors use this physical demonstration to illustrate a principle they believe applies with equal force to personal and professional achievement, that identifying and knocking over the correct initial domino, however small it might appear, can set in motion a chain reaction of consequences that eventually produces results dramatically disproportionate to the modest scale of the original action. this domino effect metaphor carries significant implications for how the authors want readers to think about goal setting and daily prioritization. rather than treating all the various tasks and goals competing for attention as roughly equivalent in importance, requiring some kind of balanced allocation of effort across all of them, the domino effect framework suggests that a small number of specific, correctly identified actions carry vastly disproportionate weight in determining eventual outcomes, while the great majority of other tasks and activities, however legitimate they might appear in isolation, contribute comparatively little to the actual trajectory of significant achievement. the authors devote considerable attention in these opening chapters to distinguishing their approach from more conventional productivity advice that emphasizes comprehensive planning, extensive to do lists, and the systematic management of many simultaneous priorities. they argue that this conventional approach, however well intentioned, actually works against the achievement of extraordinary results by diffusing attention and effort across too many competing demands, none of which receives the concentrated focus that genuinely significant achievement requires. the alternative they propose is not the abandonment of planning and goal setting altogether, but a radical narrowing of that planning process down to the identification of the single most important action available at any given time, in any given domain of a persons life. the opening chapters close by previewing the structure of the remainder of the book, explaining that before the practical application of the focusing question and the domino effect can be fully developed, the authors must first address a series of widely held but ultimately counterproductive beliefs about success and productivity that stand in the way of readers genuinely embracing the narrow, concentrated focus the book is recommending. these beliefs, which the authors term the six lies, will occupy the next several chapters, systematically dismantling each one before the book turns to the practical tools and techniques for actually applying the one thing philosophy in daily life. the practical genius of the domino effect metaphor lies precisely in its accessibility, offering a vivid, memorable image that readers can return to whenever they find themselves tempted to scatter their efforts across many simultaneous priorities rather than committing to the disciplined identification of the single, correctly chosen starting point capable of setting the entire chain in motion. the chapters that follow will systematically address the psychological and cultural obstacles that make this kind of disciplined narrowing genuinely difficult to sustain in practice, before returning to practical tools for applying the focusing question consistently across the various timeframes and domains of an actual, lived life. as readers move into the following chapters, they should keep this central image in mind, since everything that follows in the book, the systematic dismantling of the six lies and the practical tools introduced later, ultimately serves this single, central purpose, helping readers identify their own correctly chosen first domino and then providing the tools necessary to knock it over with genuine consistency and genuine focus.
