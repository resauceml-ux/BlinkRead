the second chapter of the power of now deepens the philosophical foundation of the first by addressing the question of pain directly, examining both the psychological mechanisms through which most human suffering is generated and perpetuated and the specific quality of consciousness that Tolle argues is the only genuine way out of that suffering. the chapter introduces the concept of the pain body, one of the most distinctive and most practically illuminating ideas in the entire book, as Tolles term for the accumulated residue of unprocessed emotional pain that he argues lives in the body of virtually every human being and that, when activated, exerts a powerful and largely unconscious influence over thinking, perception, and behavior. Tolle opens by distinguishing between pain that is unavoidable, the natural suffering that accompanies genuine loss, illness, and the inevitable difficulties of human life, and the additional layer of suffering that is created by the minds resistance to whatever is happening in the present moment. this resistance, the chronic refusal to accept the present moment as it actually is in favor of how the mind believes it should be, is in Tolles framework the primary generator of psychological suffering and the one that is most directly addressed by the practice of presence. the unavoidable pain of life is real and must be met honestly. the additional suffering created by resistance to that pain is optional, and its dissolution is the primary practical benefit of the kind of present moment awareness that the book teaches. the pain body, as Tolle describes it, is not metaphorical but quite literal in its operation. it is an energetic field of old emotional pain, accumulated from personal history and in some sense from the collective history of human suffering, that lives in the body as a kind of semi autonomous emotional entity with its own specific needs and behaviors. the pain body requires pain to sustain itself, and when it becomes active, usually triggered by some situation that resonates with old patterns of hurt, it essentially hijacks the thinking mind, generating thoughts and perceptions that are colored by its own emotional state and designed to produce further pain on which it can continue to feed. a particularly important insight Tolle offers about the pain body is that it actively works to prevent the kind of present moment awareness that would dissolve it, since the light of genuine conscious presence is, in his framework, precisely what the pain body cannot survive. when you become genuinely present, when you bring full, alert, non identified attention to what is actually happening in your experience in this moment, the pain body loses the unconscious identification with your thinking that allows it to operate. it becomes visible as a phenomenon in your experience rather than remaining invisible as the assumed reality of who you are, and this visibility is itself the beginning of its dissolution. the practical technique Tolle describes for working with the pain body when it is active involves a specific quality of witnessing, the ability to feel the emotional pain fully without either fighting it, running from it, or identifying with its thinking as though it were an accurate representation of reality. this witnessing is not the detached, cold observation of something unwanted from a safe distance but an alert, present, fully embodied attention that feels the pain without being consumed by it, that observes the thoughts the pain body generates without taking them seriously as reliable descriptions of the actual situation. this is a demanding practice that requires the kind of foundational presence that the first chapter introduced. the chapter closes with Tolles observation that the pain body is not a personal failing or a sign of weakness but an almost universal feature of the human condition at its current stage of psychological development. virtually everyone carries some accumulation of unprocessed emotional pain, and the specific forms that pain body takes in each individual reflect the particular history and circumstances of that persons life. what varies is not whether the pain body exists but the degree of identification with it and therefore the degree to which it unconsciously controls experience and behavior. reducing that identification through the consistent practice of conscious presence is the primary path that Tolle describes toward genuine, lasting inner freedom.
