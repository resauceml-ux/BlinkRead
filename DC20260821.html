the third chapter of the power of now begins the books more extended practical exploration of what it actually means to live in the present moment, moving beyond the foundational concept of dis identification from the thinking mind to address the specific challenges and techniques involved in actually inhabiting the now as a moment to moment way of being rather than merely as an intellectually accepted idea. Tolle opens by introducing a distinction that is central to the entire books practical teaching, the difference between clock time and what he calls psychological time. clock time is the practical use of past and future for planning, learning, and navigating the requirements of ordinary life. referring to what happened yesterday to understand something that needs to happen tomorrow is clock time, and Tolle has no objection to it. it is functional, necessary, and entirely compatible with genuine presence. psychological time, by contrast, is the mind habitual tendency to dwell in past or future not for any practical purpose but as a substitute for the present moment itself, as a way of avoiding the direct, unmediated experience of what is actually happening right now. psychological time is the source of a great deal of unnecessary suffering, anxiety about imagined futures and resentment about remembered pasts that has nothing to do with anything actually occurring in the present moment. the central claim of this section of the chapter is that genuine problems, the kind that generate real psychological suffering, almost never exist in the present moment itself. they exist in the minds projection of past patterns forward into imagined future scenarios, and in its tendency to evaluate and judge the present moment against some standard of how things should be that is generated entirely by mental concept rather than by the actual texture of present experience. when attention is genuinely in the present moment, dealing with what is actually here rather than with what the mind imagines could go wrong or what might have gone differently in the past, a remarkable quality of clarity and workability tends to emerge that the anxiety of psychological time perpetually obscures. Tolle introduces the concept of presence as something qualitatively different from ordinary concentration or deliberate attention. concentration is a mental act, the voluntary focusing of attention on a specific object or task. presence, in Tolles framework, is not an act but a quality of being, a dimension of aliveness and alert awareness that underlies and precedes all specific acts of attention. you cannot create presence through effort in the way you can create concentration through effort, because effort is itself a mental activity that belongs to the same dimension as ordinary thinking. presence is discovered rather than created, recognized as the field within which all mental activity occurs rather than as one more form of mental activity itself. the practical instruction Tolle offers for accessing this quality of presence involves the simple but genuinely challenging practice of withdrawing attention from the habitual stream of thought and redirecting it into a direct, unmediated awareness of the present moment itself, of the sensations, sounds, and aliveness of the body, of the immediate sensory field of the here and now. this redirection does not require any particular mental content or any particular emotional state. it simply requires the willingness to be here rather than to be lost in the mental narrative about here, to experience directly rather than to think about the experience. the first part of this chapter closes by addressing what Tolle considers the most important practical distinction in the entire book, the difference between using the present moment as a means to a future end, which is the orientation of most human activity, and treating the present moment as the primary reality to which all activity is subordinate. he argues that both orientations have their place but that the second, deeper orientation is the one that transforms the quality of all experience, making even ordinary activities feel alive and meaningful in a way that the endless orientation toward future gratification and future arrival simply cannot provide.
