the first of the thirteen principles that Hill identifies as essential to achieving any significant goal is desire, and he is careful from the outset to distinguish the specific kind of desire he is describing from the ordinary, diffuse wishing and hoping that most people mistake for genuine desire. the desire Hill is describing is not a preference or an aspiration. it is a consuming, obsessive, definiteness of purpose that occupies the dominant space in a persons thinking, that shapes every decision and colors every perception, that does not weaken when obstacles appear but intensifies in response to them. Hill introduces his foundational claim about desire through the extended story of Edwin Barnes and his determination to become Thomas Edison partner. Barnes had no resources and no qualifications but possessed this quality of burning, specific, unconditional desire to a degree that was immediately noticeable to one of the most perceptive observers of human nature in american history. Hill argues that Barnes desire was not simply very strong. it had a specific quality that distinguished it from ordinary ambition, it was backed by an absolute definiteness of purpose that made every alternative to its achievement feel genuinely unacceptable. the practical mechanics of desire as Hill describes them involve six specific steps that he presents as a method for transforming a mere wish into the kind of burning desire that actually produces results. the first is to fix in ones mind the exact amount of money one desires rather than a vague ambition toward wealth in general. the second is to determine exactly what one intends to give in return for the desired amount, since Hill is emphatic that there is no such thing as something for nothing and that the precise terms of value exchange must be clearly established. the third is to establish a specific date by which the desired amount will be acquired. the fourth step is to create a definite plan for carrying out the desire and to begin immediately, even if one does not yet feel fully ready or fully prepared, since the action itself tends to generate the clarity and the momentum that waiting for perfect readiness never does. the fifth step is to write out a clear, specific statement combining all of the above elements, and the sixth is to read that written statement aloud twice daily, once upon rising in the morning and once before sleeping at night, seeing and feeling and believing yourself already in possession of the desired outcome. Hill acknowledges that these steps will appear impractical or even foolish to many readers, and he addresses this reaction directly and without apology. the world, he observes, has always been hostile to new ideas and to people who refused to accept conventional limitations. the people who are most likely to dismiss these steps as unrealistic are typically those who have never genuinely put them to a rigorous test over a sustained period of time. the people who have genuinely applied them, Hill argues from his research, almost uniformly report that they produced results that exceeded whatever they had previously believed was possible. the chapter closes with a remarkable anecdote about the great composer Ludwig van Beethoven, who continued to compose music of extraordinary beauty and complexity after becoming completely deaf, a circumstance that would have ended the careers of virtually everyone else. Hill presents this as evidence of the kind of desire he is describing, a desire so consuming and so deeply identified with ones sense of purpose that even the most devastating external obstacles cannot extinguish it. the lesson is not that everyone who desires something strongly enough will achieve it regardless of circumstances, but that the quality of desire Hill describes is a necessary condition for any significant achievement, and its absence makes such achievement essentially impossible regardless of whatever other advantages a person might possess.
