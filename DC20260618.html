the third chapter shifts the focus from Marcus childhood toward the philosophical education that would come to define his approach to life, centering the discussion on one of the most distinctive and practically useful concepts in Stoic philosophy, the figure of the sage. Robertson explains that the sage represents the Stoic ideal of perfect wisdom and virtue, a theoretical figure who has achieved complete clarity of judgment, total freedom from destructive emotion, and unwavering consistency between values and action. crucially, Robertson emphasizes that the Stoics themselves generally did not claim that any actual historical person had fully achieved this state. the sage functions not as a historical claim but as a contemplative tool, a standard against which ones own progress can be measured and a model whose imagined perspective can guide difficult decisions. the chapter explores how this concept would have been introduced to Marcus during his philosophical education, likely through his various tutors in rhetoric and philosophy who exposed him to the writings and teachings of earlier Stoic thinkers. Robertson describes the broader intellectual environment of Marcus youth, a Rome in which philosophy was taken seriously as a guide to practical living rather than treated as a purely academic pursuit confined to abstract debate. wealthy and politically connected families like Marcus own routinely employed philosophical tutors, and the expectation was that a young man being prepared for public life would absorb not just rhetorical skill but genuine ethical training. a central technique that Robertson highlights in this chapter is the practice of mentally consulting the sage, or a similarly idealized figure, when facing a difficult decision. rather than relying solely on immediate impulse or untested judgment, the Stoic practitioner is encouraged to pause and imagine how a person of perfect wisdom would respond to the situation at hand. this is not mysticism. it is a practical cognitive exercise, a way of creating psychological distance from the immediate pressure of a decision and accessing a more considered, more virtuous perspective than instinct alone might provide. Robertson draws a clear and explicit parallel between this ancient technique and a range of modern psychological practices. he points to contemporary methods in which therapists encourage clients to imagine the advice of a wise, compassionate figure, whether a respected mentor, an idealized version of themselves, or simply an imagined wise advisor, as a way of accessing perspective that anxiety or anger might otherwise obscure. the chapter argues that this convergence between ancient philosophical practice and modern psychotherapeutic technique is not coincidental. both traditions, working independently across centuries, arrived at the same basic insight, that imagining an idealized observer can help a person access wisdom that their immediate emotional state makes difficult to reach. the chapter also addresses an important nuance in how the sage concept should be used. Robertson cautions against treating the sage as an impossible standard that produces only frustration and self criticism. the value of the sage lies not in achieving it but in the direction it provides, much as a distant star might guide a sailors course without the sailor ever expecting to actually reach the star itself. Marcus, Robertson suggests, understood this distinction well, using the concept of perfect wisdom as a compass for continual self improvement rather than as a source of anxiety about his own inevitable shortcomings. this chapter also begins to develop the theme that gives it its subtitle in the books structure, how to follow your values. Robertson explains that for the Stoics, living well meant living in consistent accordance with a clearly understood set of virtues, principally wisdom, justice, courage, and self discipline. the figure of the sage embodies the perfect integration of these virtues, providing a unified picture of what a fully virtuous life would look like in practice. by contemplating this figure regularly, the Stoic practitioner reinforces their own commitment to these values, making it more likely that their daily choices will align with their deeper principles rather than drifting toward convenience or impulse. Robertson also discusses how this practice connects to the broader Stoic emphasis on self examination. the philosophical tradition Marcus was being trained in placed significant weight on regular, honest reflection on ones own conduct, often through journaling or structured contemplative exercises. the sage served as a kind of measuring stick for this reflection, allowing the practitioner to ask not simply whether their actions had produced good outcomes but whether they had acted in a manner consistent with genuine virtue, regardless of the external results. the chapter closes by considering how this early exposure to the concept of the sage would shape Marcus later writings, particularly in his private notebook, the meditations, which contains numerous passages in which he reflects on the qualities of various respected figures in his own life, family members, teachers, and friends whose individual virtues he sought to emulate. Robertson suggests that this practice, listing and contemplating the virtues of real people he admired, represents a natural extension and personalization of the more abstract philosophical concept of the sage, bringing the theoretical ideal down into the concrete, lived relationships of Marcus actual life. this blending of abstract philosophical contemplation with practical, relational reflection becomes one of the defining features of Marcus particular approach to Stoicism, distinguishing his personal practice from a more purely theoretical or academic engagement with the philosophy and grounding it instead in the everyday texture of his relationships and decisions.
