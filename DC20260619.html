the fourth chapter introduces one of the most personally significant relationships in Marcus life, his adoptive brother and eventual co emperor, Lucius Verus, using the contrast between these two men as a vehicle for exploring the Stoic approach to desire and hedonism. Robertson frames the entire chapter around a famous philosophical allegory known as the choice of Hercules, a story that originated with the ancient sophist Prodicus and was later retold by Socrates, in which a young Hercules encounters two figures representing virtue and vice, each urging him to follow her particular path through life. the allegory, as Robertson explains it, presents Hercules with a vivid choice. one figure, representing vice, promises an easy and pleasurable existence, free from hardship and rich in immediate gratification. the other figure, representing virtue, offers a much harder road, full of struggle and effort, but leading ultimately to a life of genuine excellence, lasting satisfaction, and honor. Hercules, in the traditional telling, chooses the difficult path of virtue, and this choice becomes a foundational story used by generations of philosophers, including the founder of Stoicism, Zeno, to illustrate the fundamental decision that every person faces about how to live. Robertson uses this ancient allegory as a lens through which to examine the very real and very different paths chosen by Marcus and Lucius Verus, who were raised together in the household of the emperor Antoninus Pius and exposed to the same philosophical education. despite this shared upbringing, the two young men developed in starkly different directions. Marcus committed himself with increasing seriousness to the rigorous practice of stoic philosophy, while Lucius, according to the historical sources Robertson draws on, drifted toward a life characterized by excess, indulgence, and a notable lack of self discipline. the chapter provides vivid detail about Lucius reputation as a hedonist, describing his fondness for lavish parties and extravagant entertainment. Robertson recounts an episode in which the expenses for a single celebration thrown by Lucius reportedly dwarfed the annual pay of an entire Roman legion, a detail meant to underscore the scale of his excess and the contrast with Marcus own famously modest and disciplined lifestyle. this contrast is not presented merely as gossip about a colorful historical figure. it serves Robertson larger purpose of illustrating, through real historical example, the abstract choice that the Hercules allegory describes. despite these significant differences in character, Robertson explains that Marcus made the deliberate political decision to appoint Lucius as his co emperor, sharing power with him rather than ruling alone. this decision is presented as a practical application of Stoic principles in itself, reflecting Marcus recognition that Lucius had his own legitimate claim to the throne through his birth family, and that excluding him entirely could create dangerous political instability. rather than allowing personal judgment about Lucius character to override prudent political calculation, Marcus chose a path that prioritized the stability and welfare of the empire, even though it meant sharing power with someone whose values differed so sharply from his own. the chapter explores Marcus own feelings about his brother with considerable nuance. Robertson notes that despite his evident disapproval of Lucius lifestyle, Marcus expressions about him in his private writings reflect genuine affection and gratitude, alongside disappointment. this complexity reflects a broader Stoic principle that Robertson develops throughout the chapter, that virtue does not require harsh judgment or rejection of those who fall short of it. the wise person, in the Stoic framework, can recognize and even regret another person flaws while still maintaining compassion and functional relationships with them, understanding that everyone is working with their own circumstances, education, and capacity for self control. a significant portion of the chapter is devoted to unpacking the psychological mechanics of desire as understood by Stoic philosophy. Robertson explains that the Stoics did not view all desire as inherently problematic but distinguished between healthy and unhealthy forms of wanting. unhealthy desire, the kind exemplified by Lucius pursuit of pleasure and luxury, was understood as a form of misjudgment, an overvaluing of external things, like wealth, status, and physical pleasure, that are not genuinely within a person control and therefore cannot reliably provide lasting fulfillment. the Stoic alternative was not the suppression of all desire but the redirection of desire toward virtue itself, the one thing the Stoics believed could never be taken away by external circumstance. Robertson connects this ancient framework to modern psychological understanding of addiction, compulsive behavior, and the pursuit of short term pleasure at the expense of long term wellbeing, drawing parallels between Stoic techniques for examining and questioning desire and contemporary cognitive behavioral approaches to managing unhealthy patterns of wanting and consuming. the chapter suggests that Lucius story, viewed through this lens, represents not simply a moral failing but a kind of psychological pattern that modern readers might recognize in themselves or others, a pattern the Stoic framework offers specific tools for addressing. the chapter closes by reflecting on the eventual end of Lucius life and the lasting impact of this contrast on Marcus own self understanding. Robertson suggests that observing his brother example, both the genuine warmth between them and the stark differences in how they each approached life, deepened Marcus own commitment to the Stoic path he had chosen, providing him with a living, daily illustration of the choice of Hercules playing out not in ancient myth but in the actual unfolding of his own family life and imperial responsibilities.
