the second part of the third chapter continues the exploration of present moment awareness by introducing several important refinements and expansions of the practical teaching introduced in the first part. Tolle begins this section with the concept of what he calls the vertical dimension of time, distinguishing it from the horizontal dimension that most people inhabit as their default relationship with temporal experience. the horizontal dimension is the familiar flow from past to future, the linear sequence of events in which most people locate their sense of identity, continuity, and meaning. the vertical dimension, by contrast, is the depth of the present moment itself, the quality of presence and aliveness that is always available in the now but that the horizontal orientation to time tends to completely obscure. Tolle argues that what most spiritual and contemplative traditions have pointed toward, however differently they have expressed it, is this vertical dimension of the present moment, the recognition that beneath the horizontal flow of events and experiences there is a quality of being that is not itself moving, not itself changing, not itself the product of past causes or the raw material of future effects. this quality is what Tolle calls presence or being, and he argues that genuine contact with it produces a transformation in the quality of experience that cannot be achieved through any amount of horizontal improvement, any accumulation of better experiences, more successful outcomes, or more favorable circumstances arranged along the timeline of ones life. the practical dimension of this teaching in the second part of the chapter focuses particularly on the use of the body as an anchor to present moment awareness. Tolle argues that the body, far from being an obstacle to spiritual development or something to be transcended in favor of purely mental or spiritual experience, is actually one of the most reliable and most accessible gateways to the present moment available. the body always exists in the present, it has no capacity for inhabiting past or future in the way the thinking mind does, and by bringing conscious attention into the body, feeling the aliveness and energy within it, a person can reliably access a dimension of present awareness that the thinking mind cannot provide. this practice of body awareness connects to a broader principle Tolle introduces here, the relationship between the quality of attention and the depth of presence accessible. when attention is diffuse, scattered among the various contents of habitual thinking, the quality of presence is correspondingly shallow and intermittent. when attention is gathered and brought into focused, embodied awareness, the quality of presence deepens correspondingly. the body provides a specific, concrete, always available object for this gathered attention, one that has the particular advantage of being inherently present in a way that purely mental objects of attention are not. the chapter also addresses the important distinction between genuine acceptance of the present moment and what Tolle carefully distinguishes from it as passive resignation. acceptance, in his framework, does not mean approval of everything that is happening, passive surrender to circumstances that could be changed, or the abandonment of all effort toward improvement. it means the fundamental orientation of recognizing what is actually happening in the present moment as the starting point for all engagement, rather than beginning from a position of resistance to what is that immediately colors all perception and all action with the additional suffering of that resistance. from genuine acceptance, effective, energized action is entirely possible and often arises naturally. from resistance, action tends to be reactive, anxious, and less effective. Tolle closes this section by reflecting on the paradoxical quality of the present moment when it is experienced with full, undefended presence rather than through the filter of habitual thought and psychological time. the now, fully inhabited, has a quality of completeness and sufficiency that the perpetual orientation toward past and future never provides, because past and future are defined precisely by their incompleteness, by the absence of something that was or the anticipation of something that is yet to arrive. only the present moment contains everything that is actually here, and in that completeness lies a quality that Tolle describes as a close approximation of what the contemplative traditions have called eternity.
