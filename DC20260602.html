Law thirty five of the forty eight laws of power is about the mastery of timing. Robert Greene argues that even the most brilliant strategy, the most well resourced plan, and the most skillfully executed action can fail if they arrive at the wrong moment. conversely, a modest action taken at exactly the right moment can achieve results that far exceed what the action itself would seem capable of producing. timing is the invisible variable that determines whether all the other variables add up to success or failure, and the person who has mastered the art of reading and selecting the right moment has an advantage that is almost impossible for those who have not to overcome. the opening insight of Greene's discussion is that time is not uniform. not all moments are equally favorable for action. some moments are charged with possibility, when circumstances have aligned in ways that make a particular action likely to succeed. other moments are hostile to that same action, when forces are arrayed against it or when the people involved are not in the right state of mind to respond as you need them to. the person who cannot distinguish between these types of moments will experience their actions as unpredictably successful or unsuccessful, sometimes things work and sometimes they do not, but they will not understand why. the person who can read the moment accurately will find that their success rate rises dramatically, not because their capabilities have changed but because they have learned to act when the conditions are favorable and wait when they are not. Greene distinguishes between several different relationships to time that characterize different types of actors. the first type is the impulsive actor, the person who acts when the feeling is strong rather than when the moment is right. this type is driven by emotion and by the internal pressure of their own desires. they want things to happen and they push for them before the external conditions are ready. their actions often produce premature results that either fail outright or create resistance and backlash that would not have appeared if they had waited. the feeling of impatience and urgency that drives them is internal, not a reading of the external situation, and external reality does not bend easily to internal pressure. the second type is the passive waiter, the person who is always preparing but never acting, always watching for the right moment but finding reasons why the current moment is not quite right. this type avoids the failures of premature action but also avoids the successes that timely action produces. they wait so long that the window of opportunity closes before they have moved. their caution is real but it is not strategic, it is anxiety masquerading as patience. true patience is different. it involves active engagement with the situation, careful observation of how things are developing, and a genuine readiness to act the moment the conditions align. it is the patience of the hunter who waits motionlessly for exactly the right moment to make the decisive move, not the patience of the person who cannot bring themselves to act. the third type is the master of timing, the person who has developed the ability to read the situation, to sense when momentum is building in a particular direction, to feel when resistance is weakening or when an opponent is at their most vulnerable, and to act in that precise window with everything they have. this type is capable of both patience and decisiveness, but they are not using either as a default. they are using both instrumentally, as tools deployed in response to an accurate reading of what the moment requires. Greene also discusses the specific skill of reading the mood of the people involved in a situation. the right moment for a request, a negotiation, or a confrontation is not just a matter of external circumstances. it also depends on the internal state of the people you are dealing with. the same request made to the same person in two different emotional states can produce radically different responses. the person who is in a good mood, who feels secure and appreciated, who is not under unusual stress, is far more likely to respond generously to a request than the same person when they are anxious, pressured, or distracted. reading these emotional conditions and timing your action to coincide with the most favorable state is itself a major dimension of the art of timing. there is also a strategic dimension to timing that relates to the flow of events in competitive situations. moments arise in any contest when the opponent is off balance, when their resources are committed elsewhere, when they have just suffered a setback and are regrouping, when their attention is divided, or when their support has been weakened. these are the moments when action produces the greatest return. the person who recognizes these windows and acts within them, rather than waiting for a moment of perfect strength and preparation on their own side, often wins contests that their objective capabilities would not have seemed to favor. Greene draws on historical military campaigns to illustrate this principle. the commanders who changed history were not always the ones with the largest armies or the most resources. they were often the ones who were best at reading the flow of events and identifying the moment when a decisive action would have a disproportionate effect. this ability to sense the decisive moment and act within it, rather than before or after, is what Greene calls the art of timing in its fullest expression. there is also a psychological aspect of this law that relates to how time itself is experienced in moments of crisis or competition. when things are going badly, time seems to slow and every moment of continuing difficulty feels interminable. this psychological pressure to act, to do something to change the situation, is one of the most powerful drivers of premature action. the disciplined practitioner of timing recognizes this pressure for what it is, an internal state rather than an accurate reading of the external situation, and resists it when waiting is the strategically correct choice. in conclusion, law thirty five teaches that time is an active ingredient in every plan and every action, not merely the background against which things happen but a variable that must be read, managed, and used with the same care and skill as any other resource. the master of timing is neither impulsive nor passively patient. they are actively engaged with the flow of events, watching for the moments when action will have the greatest possible effect, and ready to move decisively when those moments arrive. this mastery, more than almost any other single capability, separates those who achieve what they set out to achieve from those who find that their efforts produce disappointing and unpredictable results.
