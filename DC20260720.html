the sixth habit is presented by Covey as the natural and highest expression of all the habits that preceded it, the creative outcome that becomes possible when proactivity, personal vision, effective self management, win win thinking, and empathic listening are all operating together in a relationship or a team. the habit is synergy, a word that Covey uses to describe the creative cooperation that produces outcomes genuinely greater than the sum of what the individual contributors could have achieved independently, the recognition that one plus one can equal three, or ten, or a hundred, when the right conditions of trust, communication, and creative openness are in place. Covey grounds the concept of synergy in the simple observation that different people see the world differently, and that these differences, which are so often experienced as sources of friction, conflict, and interpersonal difficulty, are actually the very raw material from which genuinely creative and innovative solutions are built. the person who sees the situation from one angle and the person who sees it from a completely different angle together possess between them a more complete and more accurate picture of the situation than either could have assembled from their individual perspective alone. but this complementary richness is only accessible if both parties are genuinely open to what the other sees rather than defending their own perspective as the only accurate or legitimate one. the crucial distinction that Covey draws at the heart of this chapter is between three different levels of attitude toward human differences. the first and lowest level is simple tolerance, the grudging acknowledgment that other people see things differently and the decision to coexist with that fact without conflict. tolerance does not celebrate difference or seek to learn from it. it simply accepts it as an unavoidable feature of shared life and attempts to manage the friction it creates. the second level is acceptance, which goes somewhat further in recognizing that different perspectives may be genuinely valid and valuable even when they differ significantly from your own. but the highest level, and the only level from which genuine synergy can emerge, is celebration, the active valuing of differences as contributions to a richer, more complete understanding of reality than any single perspective could provide. Covey introduces the concept of the third alternative to describe what becomes possible when people operate from this celebratory orientation toward difference. in most discussions and negotiations, the parties begin from their respective positions and the conversation involves attempting to move the other party toward your position, or finding a compromise somewhere in the middle that partially satisfies both parties without fully satisfying either. the third alternative is something categorically different, a creative solution that neither party had originally conceived but that emerges from the genuine, open-minded, collaborative engagement of people who trust each other enough to be vulnerable about their uncertainty and curious about what the other person knows and sees that they do not. the conditions that make synergy possible are not accidental. they are the direct product of the habits that have come before, particularly the trust that builds between people who consistently demonstrate integrity in their own conduct, the openness that comes from genuinely practicing empathic listening, and the creative energy that is released when both parties approach an interaction with genuine win win intention rather than competitive or defensive orientation. Covey argues that most people have experienced moments of genuine synergy, perhaps in an exciting conversation that produced a solution neither party could have reached alone, but that few people have the understanding or the skill to create these conditions reliably and intentionally. the chapter also addresses the internal synergy that comes from integrating the different aspects of your own nature, your analytical and creative capacities, your emotional and rational responses, your individual goals and your responsibilities to others. genuine personal effectiveness, Covey suggests, requires not the suppression of any aspect of the self but the creative integration of all of them into a coherent, whole person approach to life and its challenges. Covey closes by noting that synergy requires courage as well as skill. it requires the willingness to be genuinely uncertain, to not know the answer before the conversation begins, to trust that the collaborative process of open minded engagement with another perspective will produce something better than what either party could have created defensively alone. in a world that often rewards certainty and punishes vulnerability, this requires a genuine investment of trust and a willingness to discover rather than to merely confirm what you already believed.
