the opening chapter of think and grow rich does not begin with a technique or a strategy. it begins with a secret. Napoleon Hill, who spent twenty five years studying the lives and methods of more than five hundred of the most successful individuals in american history at the commission of the steel magnate Andrew Carnegie, claims that embedded within the pages of this book is a secret that has been used by virtually every person who has achieved lasting success in any field. he declines to name the secret directly, arguing that each reader must discover it for themselves, that the act of discovery is itself part of the process of making it genuinely ones own. the central story Hill uses to ground this opening is his own encounter with Carnegie, which occurred when Hill was sent as a young reporter to interview the already legendary industrialist. Carnegie used the interview to propose something unusual, that Hill should spend the next twenty years of his life studying the philosophy of success, interviewing the most successful people in america, and synthesizing what he discovered into a form that would be accessible to ordinary people who lacked the connections and resources that had given those successful individuals their opportunities. Carnegie offered no financial support for this project. he offered only his endorsement and his willingness to introduce Hill to the extraordinary network of powerful men he knew. Hill accepted, and the result of those twenty five years of research is what the book presents. but before any of the specific principles are introduced, Hill is at pains to establish a foundational premise without which none of the subsequent material can be properly understood. that premise is the reality and power of thought as a force capable of producing material results in the world. Hill argues, drawing on what he presents as the accumulated evidence of hundreds of conversations with spectacularly successful people, that the dominating thoughts a person holds in their mind tend to translate themselves into physical reality through mechanisms that are not always fully understood but are nonetheless consistently observable. this opening chapter also introduces the story of Edwin Barnes, a young man who arrived at Thomas Edison laboratory in nineteen hundred with no money, no connections, no impressive education, and no obvious qualifications, but with an absolutely clear and burning desire to become a business partner of the most famous inventor in america. Edison himself noted that Barnes had something in his eyes that suggested he would get what he came for. what Barnes had was not talent, not luck, and not connections, but the quality of focused, persistent desire that Hill will identify throughout the book as the single most indispensable ingredient of any significant achievement. Hill also introduces his own son Blair, who was born without ears and was told by medical authorities that he would never be able to hear or speak normally. Hill refused to accept this verdict and raised his son with the belief that he would find a way to hear despite his physical condition. Blair eventually did develop the ability to hear through a remarkable combination of bone conduction and intense focused attention, and later became an advocate for others with similar conditions, a story Hill presents as personal testimony to the power of the beliefs we hold about what is possible. the chapter closes by insisting that the philosophy presented in the book is not theory but practical, tested wisdom distilled from the actual experiences of people who achieved extraordinary results starting from ordinary or even severely disadvantaged circumstances. the secret, Hill tells the reader, is not withheld to be clever. it is withheld because it can only be recognized and claimed by a mind that is ready to receive it, and that readiness is itself something that develops through genuine engagement with the ideas that follow rather than through passive reading or cursory attention. the invitation the chapter extends is not simply to read but to engage, to reflect, and to remain alert for the moment when the secret makes itself recognizable within the context of ones own life and ones own deepest desires.
