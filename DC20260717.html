the third habit represents the practical, behavioral expression of the first two habits, translating the principle of proactivity and the vision established by beginning with the end in mind into the concrete daily and weekly management of time and priorities. Covey describes the first habit as the awareness that you are the programmer of your own life, the second as the writing of the program through your personal mission statement and values, and the third as the actual running of the program, the moment to moment and day to day management of choices in a way that is consistent with your deepest values and highest priorities. the centerpiece of this chapter is what Covey calls the time management matrix, a framework for categorizing all activities according to two key dimensions, urgency and importance. urgency refers to whether an activity demands immediate attention, whether there is a deadline or external pressure requiring a response right now. importance refers to whether an activity contributes to your mission, values, and high priority goals. by combining these two dimensions, Covey generates four quadrants that describe the four fundamental categories of how people spend their time. quadrant one contains activities that are both urgent and important, genuine crises, pressing deadlines, and emergencies that require immediate attention. Covey acknowledges that some time in quadrant one is unavoidable and that handling genuine crises is a real part of effective living. but he observes that many people spend the majority of their time in this quadrant, living in a state of continuous crisis management and firefighting that leaves them exhausted, reactive, and unable to invest meaningfully in anything beyond the immediate emergency. quadrant two is the heart of effective personal management in Covey framework. it contains activities that are important but not urgent, things like building and maintaining relationships, personal development and education, long term planning, exercise and health maintenance, and the preparation and prevention work that reduces the number of quadrant one crises that eventually require emergency attention. because these activities are not urgent, they rarely generate their own pressure demanding immediate attention. they can always wait until tomorrow, and for most people they do wait, indefinitely, crowded out by the urgent demands of quadrant one and the attractive distractions of quadrant three and four. quadrant three contains activities that are urgent but not genuinely important, the ringing phone that turns out to be a trivial matter, the meeting that addresses someone else priorities rather than your own, the email that feels pressing but contributes nothing to your actual mission. Covey notes that people who mistake urgency for importance end up spending enormous amounts of time in quadrant three believing they are being highly productive, when in fact they are primarily serving other people agendas and priorities rather than their own genuine goals. quadrant four contains activities that are neither urgent nor important, pure time wasters and escapist behaviors that provide neither genuine value nor even genuine rest and renewal. Covey recommends shrinking time in quadrant four as much as possible and redirecting that time toward quadrant two investment instead. the most important practical recommendation in this chapter is the shift from daily to weekly planning as the fundamental rhythm of effective time management. Covey argues that the week is a more natural and useful planning unit than the day, because it allows you to maintain perspective across all the major roles you inhabit simultaneously, as parent, professional, friend, community member, and person committed to your own development. each week, Covey recommends identifying the two or three most important activities in each of your key roles and scheduling time for those activities before filling in the remaining space with other demands. the chapter closes by addressing the courage required to actually implement this habit, the courage to say no to urgent but unimportant requests in order to protect time for quadrant two investment. Covey argues that this is ultimately the most important time management word available, and that the ability to deploy it confidently and consistently, grounded in the clarity of your personal mission and values, is what separates those who manage their time effectively from those who allow the urgent demands of others to perpetually crowd out what matters most.
