Law thirty nine of the forty eight laws of power is about the strategic use of emotion in conflict. Robert Greene teaches that anger and emotional agitation are enemies of clear thinking and effective action. the person who becomes angry, flustered, or emotionally reactive in a competitive situation loses a dimension of their strategic capability precisely at the moment when they most need it. they become predictable, they make mistakes, and they reveal information about their vulnerabilities that a calm and observant opponent can use against them. the strategic application of this law works in two directions simultaneously. first, you must protect yourself from emotional agitation. second, you can deliberately provoke your opponents into it. the central metaphor of the law is vivid and instructive. the skilled fisherman does not wait for calm waters and then hope the fish will casually swim into their net. they stir up the water, create turbulence, and drive the fish into a state of agitation and disorientation that makes them far easier to catch. the same principle applies in human competition. an opponent who is calm, settled, and fully in possession of their faculties is much harder to outmaneuver than one who is irritated, frustrated, or emotionally reactive. by identifying what angers or unsettles your opponent and touching those sensitivities deliberately, you create the turbulence that makes them easier to catch. Greene begins by observing that anger is one of the most powerful destroyers of effective thinking. when a person becomes genuinely angry, their cognitive resources narrow. their focus becomes fixed on the source and the object of their anger rather than on the broader strategic picture. they lose the flexibility and the breadth of perspective that good decision making requires. they become reactive rather than proactive, responding to what is happening rather than shaping what will happen. and reactive people, precisely because they are driven by what has already occurred, are always slightly behind the person who is setting the terms of the encounter. the practical technique for applying this law involves the careful identification of what your opponent is most sensitive to. every person has a specific set of triggers, situations, topics, or behaviors that reliably produce an emotional response that disrupts their composure. some people cannot bear to be publicly contradicted. others become reactive when their competence is questioned. some are particularly sensitive to disrespect of a certain kind, others to being kept waiting or ignored. the person who has taken the time to understand their opponent's specific emotional landscape can touch the right trigger at the right moment and watch the opponent's strategic capacity deteriorate. the trigger does not need to be and often should not be an obvious or dramatic provocation. the most effective version of this technique is the indirect touch, the casual remark, the seemingly innocent observation, or the minor piece of behavior that just grazes the opponent's sensitivity without appearing deliberate. a deliberate provocation can be recognized as such and resisted, at least by a disciplined opponent. but an indirect one, especially one that can be plausibly attributed to casualness or thoughtlessness, is much harder to defend against. the opponent who feels the prick but cannot point to a clear act of aggression finds themselves in the frustrating position of being reactive to something they cannot easily name. Greene also discusses the role of timing in this technique. the optimal moment to touch an opponent's emotional triggers is typically just before a situation in which they need to perform at their best. a negotiation, a public presentation, a competitive encounter, any situation in which the quality of their thinking will directly determine the outcome, is made much more favorable for you if the opponent enters it already emotionally disturbed. the disturbance that might have dissipated over time remains present because the timing left them no space to recover, and they bring that disturbance into the very moment when their clarity matters most. the other side of this law is the management of your own emotional state. Greene is very clear that this technique only works if you can maintain your own composure while your opponent loses theirs. the moment you yourself become emotionally reactive, you have undone the strategic advantage and created a situation where both parties are operating below their cognitive best. this symmetry benefits the stronger party in the objective sense, and if you are not obviously the stronger party, losing your composure is particularly damaging. the discipline of remaining calm when provoked is therefore not just a matter of personal virtue but a strategic imperative. Greene draws on historical examples of leaders and strategists who used the instigation of emotional reactions as a consistent tool. in military history, feints and provocations designed to make an enemy commander react in anger rather than careful deliberation have changed the outcomes of countless battles. the commander who responds in anger to a feint commits forces at the wrong moment, in the wrong place, for the wrong reason, because their emotional response has overridden their strategic judgment. and the commander who provoked that response knew exactly what would happen because they understood the psychology of the person they were dealing with. there is also a social and interpersonal dimension to this law. in personal relationships and professional environments, the person who remains unruffled in the face of provocation commands a specific kind of respect. they appear strong, self possessed, and in control. and because they do not respond to emotional bait, they deprive those who would manipulate them of the tool they most rely on. this unrufflability is itself a form of power, independent of any offensive application. it signals to everyone around you that you cannot be easily moved, and that signal discourages the kinds of manipulation and provocation that more reactive people regularly face. in conclusion, law thirty nine teaches that in any competitive situation, emotion is a variable that can be managed strategically. by protecting your own composure and by learning to touch the emotional triggers of your opponents at precisely the right moments, you create asymmetries of clarity and reactivity that favor you decisively. the calm person wins over the agitated one not merely because calm is aesthetically preferable but because it is strategically superior. the still water catches nothing. the stirred waters reveal everything.
