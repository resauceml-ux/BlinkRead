the fifth chapter addresses a principle that Hill considers frequently misunderstood and that he believes accounts for a significant proportion of the gap between people of genuine ability who fail to achieve financial success and those who achieve it reliably. that principle is specialized knowledge, and the misunderstanding Hill is addressing is the common belief that the acquisition of general education, the broad, comprehensive knowledge that formal schooling typically provides, is sufficient preparation for financial achievement. Hill argues firmly and repeatedly that it is not. the distinction Hill draws is between knowledge that sits inertly in the mind as information and knowledge that has been organized and applied toward specific, definite purposes. a professor may possess more raw information about a given subject than almost anyone in their society. yet professors as a group are not particularly known for their financial success, and Hill notes this fact as evidence that the mere possession of knowledge, however extensive, does not by itself produce financial results. what produces financial results is the organization of knowledge into practical plans and the application of those plans through persistent, coordinated action toward a specific goal. Hill is also at pains to distinguish between personally possessing specialized knowledge and knowing how to find, organize, and use the specialized knowledge of others. this distinction is particularly important in the context of his mastermind principle, which will be addressed in more detail in a later chapter, but which he previews here by noting that Henry Ford, one of the most successful businessmen in history, built his extraordinary achievement not on personal mastery of every technical and commercial field his enterprise required but on the ability to surround himself with experts in each required domain and to ask them the right questions at the right times. the chapter provides practical guidance on where and how to acquire the specialized knowledge that any particular goal requires. Hill identifies several sources, including formal education of the targeted, specific variety rather than the broad general kind, practical experience gained through direct engagement with the relevant field, self education through books, courses, and concentrated study in the specific area of required competence, and the mastermind group, a coordinated alliance with other individuals whose combined knowledge covers the entire domain that any individual member might lack. Hill also addresses the question of whether specialized knowledge alone is sufficient. it is not. specialized knowledge is a necessary but not sufficient condition for the kind of success Hill is describing. it must be combined with definiteness of purpose, a burning desire for the specific outcome being pursued, persistent organized planning, and above all the quality of faith in ones own ability to achieve the desired result that Hill identified as so essential in the preceding chapter. without these accompanying elements, even the most impressive specialized knowledge tends to remain unused or to be applied ineffectively without the sustained, motivated, coordinated effort that genuine achievement requires. the chapter closes with an observation about the accessibility of specialized knowledge in modern society, Hill noting that the means for acquiring virtually any form of specialized knowledge that any goal requires are more widely available than most people recognize or take advantage of. the limiting factor in the vast majority of cases is not the unavailability of the necessary knowledge but the absence of the definiteness of purpose and the organized plan that would direct a person toward acquiring the specific knowledge they need rather than simply accumulating information in a general, unfocused way that never gets organized into the practical force capable of producing results.
