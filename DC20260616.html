the book opens not with a beginning but with an ending. Donald Robertson chooses to introduce Marcus Aurelius through the final hours of his life, in the year one hundred and eighty, in a military camp on the Danube frontier, as the emperor lies dying of what historians now believe was the antonine plague. this is a deliberate choice. Robertson wants the reader to understand from the very first page that this is not simply a biography. it is a meditation on mortality, framed by the life of a man who spent decades practicing exactly the kind of calm acceptance that death demands. the scene Robertson constructs is vivid and intimate. Marcus, weakened by illness, surrounded by his generals and his son, prepares to die far from Rome, in the cold north, among soldiers rather than senators. this setting itself carries meaning. Marcus did not die in luxury or comfort. he died doing his duty, commanding troops against the Germanic tribes who threatened the empires northern border, a task he had returned to again and again throughout his reign despite his preference for philosophy and quiet contemplation. the contrast between what Marcus might have wanted for himself and what duty required of him runs through the entire chapter and indeed through the entire book. Robertson describes Marcus turning to his guard in his final moments and whispering a phrase that has become one of the most quoted lines associated with the emperor, go to the rising sun, for i am already setting. the guard, young and uncertain, does not know how to respond. this small human moment, a dying man trying to comfort or instruct a frightened soldier, captures something essential about Marcus character. even in his final hours, his attention turns outward, toward duty, toward others, rather than collapsing entirely into his own suffering. the chapter also introduces Commodus, Marcus son and chosen successor, in a way that foreshadows the tragedy to come. Robertson notes that Commodus, watching his fathers withered body, feels not inspired admiration but fear and disgust. he complains about the risk of contracting the plague himself and expresses his eagerness to leave the grim northern camp and return to the comforts of Rome. Marcus, even as he is dying, must manage his sons impatience, asking him to wait just a little longer before departing, and ordering his soldiers to protect Commodus from any suspicion that he might have hastened his fathers death. this is a poignant moment of foreshadowing. Commodus, history tells us, will become one of Romes most notorious emperors, abandoning much of what his father stood for. Robertson uses this opening scene to introduce the central concept that will organize the entire book, the stoic practice of contemplating death, sometimes referred to by the Latin phrase memento mori, remember that you must die. for the Stoics, this was not a morbid or depressing exercise. it was a tool for living well. by regularly reflecting on the certainty and unpredictability of death, the Stoic practitioner learns to value the present moment, to avoid wasting time on trivial concerns, and to focus energy on living virtuously rather than chasing external rewards that death will eventually render meaningless. the chapter explains that Marcus practiced this kind of reflection throughout his life, not just at its end. his personal notebook, later known as the meditations, contains repeated reflections on mortality, on the brevity of fame, on the way that even the greatest emperors and conquerors of history are eventually forgotten. Robertson argues that this lifelong practice is precisely what allowed Marcus to face his actual death with the composure that the historical sources describe. the calm of his final hours was not a sudden gift. it was the product of decades of philosophical training, applied finally to the ultimate test. Robertson also uses this opening chapter to introduce his own personal connection to the material. he explains that he came to write this book partly out of his own confrontation with mortality, following the death of his father, which led him to study philosophy, religion, and eventually psychology in search of answers about loss and meaning. this personal thread, woven into the historical narrative, signals to the reader that the book will not be a purely academic exercise. it will attempt to connect ancient wisdom directly to modern emotional experience, drawing on Robertsons training as a cognitive behavioral psychotherapist to show how Stoic ideas anticipate and complement modern psychological practice. the chapter closes by setting up the structure of the rest of the book. each subsequent chapter, Robertson explains, will pair a dramatic episode from Marcus life with a corresponding lesson from Stoic philosophy, moving chronologically forward from his childhood through his education, his rise to power, and the great trials of his reign, before finally returning, in the books last chapter, to the theme of death with which it began. this circular structure mirrors the Stoic understanding of life itself as a cycle that must be faced with the same equanimity from beginning to end. by ending the opening chapter with Marcus quiet death, surrounded by uncertainty about what will follow under his sons rule, Robertson leaves the reader with a powerful and unresolved tension. the wisdom Marcus embodied did not guarantee a good outcome for the empire he left behind. but it did guarantee, as far as anything can be guaranteed, that he himself met his end with dignity, clarity, and an absence of the fear that so often accompanies the final moments of a human life. this is the promise the book extends to its readers, not immunity from death or hardship, but the possibility of facing both with the same steady mind that sustained one of historys most respected rulers.
