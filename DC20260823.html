the fourth chapter examines the specific psychological mechanisms through which the thinking mind resists and avoids the present moment, providing what Tolle considers an essential map of the territory for anyone attempting to practice present moment awareness with any consistency. the chapter opens with what Tolle regards as a crucial insight for understanding the difficulty most people encounter in this practice, the recognition that the resistance to the present moment is not accidental or incidental but is actually built into the structure of what he calls the ego, the false sense of self that is constructed entirely from mental content and that cannot survive the kind of clear, identity dissolving presence that genuine inhabiting of the now produces. Tolle explains the ego as the false identity that most people mistake for who they are, the accumulated structure of self concept built from memories, stories, opinions, judgments, and habitual patterns of thought and reaction that the mind has generated over a lifetime and that it continuously reinforces through the act of compulsive thinking. the ego is not the same as a healthy sense of individuality or personal agency. it is specifically the structure of identification with thought itself, the belief that the voice in the head is who you are, that the mental commentary on experience is more real than the experience itself, and that the past and future in which that commentary primarily lives are more substantial than the present moment that the mind is so expert at vacating. because the ego is built from time, from past experiences interpreted and projected into imagined futures, the present moment represents a direct threat to its continuity. in genuine presence, the ego has no material to work with. there is no past to nurse and no future to worry about, and therefore no ego structure to be maintained and defended. this is why the thinking mind, operating on behalf of the ego, develops a remarkably sophisticated repertoire of strategies for avoiding the now, for pulling attention away from direct present experience and redirecting it into the safer territory of memory, anticipation, judgment, and narrative. the two primary strategies Tolle identifies are dwelling in the past and projecting into the future. the past is mined for grievances, regrets, self justifications, and pleasant nostalgia, all of which provide the ego with material for its continuous self construction project. the future is populated with worries, fantasies, plans, and anticipated satisfactions that keep attention perpetually located somewhere other than here. both orientations feel entirely natural and even productive to the mind engaged in them, since the mind genuinely believes that reviewing the past and planning the future is more useful than simply being present to what is actually happening now. Tolle challenges this belief directly, arguing that most of what the mind does in the name of productive past review and future planning is actually ego maintenance and pain body feeding disguised as practical activity. the chapter also introduces a particularly illuminating observation about the ego relationship to problems. Tolle argues that the ego actually needs problems to sustain itself, since problems provide the ego with a sense of importance, urgency, and purpose that the simple experience of being present would not. a person fully identified with their ego does not genuinely want their problems to be resolved, because resolution would leave them without the sense of embattled importance that the problems provide. this is why the same problems tend to recur in different forms throughout a life, and why genuine resolution often requires not just external action but a fundamental shift in the quality of inner presence brought to the situation. the practical recommendation Tolle offers for working with these avoidance strategies is characteristically simple. when you notice that the mind has drifted into past dwelling or future projection, simply notice. without judgment, without self criticism, without turning the noticing itself into another mental project, simply observe that thinking has gone somewhere other than here and gently bring attention back to the present moment. this returning, done hundreds or thousands of times without frustration or self judgment, gradually weakens the habits of avoidance and strengthens the capacity for genuine presence that underlies all the transformations the book describes.
