the fourteenth chapter presents what Hill describes as the apex of his entire philosophy, the principle that he considers the most difficult to understand and communicate but also the most profound in its implications for what becomes possible for anyone who develops it fully. this is the sixth sense, which Hill defines as that portion of the subconscious mind referred to as creative imagination, through which infinite intelligence communicates voluntarily, without any effort from or request by the individual. it is the faculty through which hunches and inspirations flash into the mind, the inner voice that warns of impending danger in time to avert it and whispers of opportunities before they have become obvious to the rational mind. Hill is candid from the outset that the sixth sense is the one principle in his framework that he cannot reduce to a simple, mechanistic set of instructions, and that the reader who has not first developed the other twelve principles through consistent application will almost certainly be unable to understand what he is describing in this chapter from a position of direct experience rather than merely theoretical acknowledgment. the sixth sense is not a starting point but a destination, the place that the disciplined, consistent application of all the preceding principles gradually makes accessible to those willing to do the sustained inner work they require. the most vivid and most memorable practical exercise that Hill describes in this chapter is what he calls his imaginary council of invisible counselors, a technique he developed for his own personal use and practiced for many years. the technique involves holding imaginary meetings in ones mind with the mental images of nine historical figures chosen for their specific qualities of mind and character, men whose examples Hill wished to absorb and integrate into his own thinking and behavior. his chosen council included Abraham Lincoln for his qualities of integrity and humanity, Napoleon Bonaparte for his capacity for strategic decisiveness, Thomas Edison for his creativity and persistence, and six others representing different dimensions of human excellence. Hill practiced these imaginary meetings each night before sleep, presiding over the council and asking each member for their perspective on the specific challenges and decisions he was currently facing. he notes with characteristic directness that he is fully aware of how this practice sounds to a practically minded reader and that he is not claiming these imaginary figures possessed any genuine knowledge they would otherwise have lacked. what he is claiming is that the practice of deliberately and vividly inhabiting the perspective of individuals whose qualities he admired gradually influenced his own thinking, behavior, and intuitive responses in ways that he could not have achieved through purely rational study or through simple admiration from a distance. the chapter connects the sixth sense to the broader framework of mind vibration and thought reception described in the previous chapter, presenting it as the highest state of receptivity that a fully developed, fully stimulated human mind can achieve. when desire is burning, faith is complete, the subconscious is deeply impressed with a clear definite purpose, and all the other principles are operating in coordination, the mind reaches a state in which the creative imagination begins functioning as a genuine receiving instrument for ideas and guidance that transcend what ordinary reasoning and experience would make available. Hill closes by describing the sixth sense not as a mystical gift reserved for extraordinary individuals but as a natural faculty of the human mind that lies dormant in most people because most people never develop the other mental faculties to the degree required to activate it. it is the final prize earned by the patient, persistent application of a complete philosophy of achievement, the point at which inner development reaches such a degree of refinement that the individual mind begins to access something larger than itself, receiving contributions to its creative work from sources that defy ordinary explanation but that the testimony of extraordinary achievers consistently confirms as real.
