Law twenty six of the forty eight laws of power is about the art of maintaining an unblemished reputation while still doing what power requires. Robert Greene teaches that in the world of power, many of the actions necessary to advance and protect your position are the kinds of actions that, if associated with you directly, would damage your reputation and make enemies. firing people, delivering bad news, enforcing unpopular rules, playing rivals against each other, these are the dirty work of power. and those who do this dirty work openly, who allow themselves to be directly associated with every unpleasant decision, find that over time their reputation accumulates damage that undermines their position. the answer is not to avoid the work but to keep your hands clean while still getting it done. the core technique Greene describes involves the use of intermediaries. throughout history, the most effective wielders of power have understood that the direct path is not always the best one. when something unpleasant must be done, it is better to have someone else do it, or at least to appear as though someone else is responsible. this creates a buffer between you and the unpleasant act, preserving your reputation while still allowing the necessary work to get done. the person through whom the act is carried out becomes the face of it, the one who is remembered as having done the dirty thing, while you remain associated with the larger and more elevated purposes that the dirty work served. Greene calls this the use of scapegoats and cat's paws. a scapegoat is someone who absorbs blame for something that went wrong, whether or not they were actually responsible. a cat's paw is someone who is used to accomplish a task without necessarily understanding that they are being used. both serve the function of keeping the person who truly directed the action at a distance from its consequences. this sounds ruthless, and in some applications it is. but Greene frames it as a practical recognition of how power operates in any complex social environment. blame and credit do not flow naturally to those who deserve them. they flow in the directions that serve existing interests and narratives. the wise practitioner of power understands this and manages the flow deliberately. the maintenance of a clean reputation is not merely about avoiding blame. it is about projecting a consistent image of fairness, competence, and good intention that makes people willing to give you their trust and their loyalty. a person whose reputation has been stained by association with cruelty, manipulation, or failure finds that this stain colors everything they do subsequently. every decision they make is viewed through the lens of the earlier damage. every mistake is seen as consistent with the negative pattern. but a person with a clean reputation benefits from the opposite effect. their mistakes are attributed to circumstance rather than character. their successes are magnified by the existing positive impression. the clean reputation is a multiplier that amplifies everything good and cushions everything bad. Greene also discusses the technique of using time as a buffer. sometimes the dirty work does not require a human intermediary at all. it requires only patience. if you wait long enough, circumstances will arrange themselves in ways that accomplish what you needed without requiring your direct intervention. a rival who is allowed to continue making mistakes will eventually destroy themselves. a project that is clearly headed for failure will fail on its own schedule without requiring you to actively undermine it. the person who understands this can sometimes achieve what they need through strategic inaction while their reputation remains entirely untouched. there is also a psychological dimension to this law that Greene explores carefully. people are deeply uncomfortable with those they believe have dirty hands. there is a moral intuition that those who do unpleasant things, even when those things are necessary, are somehow tainted. this intuition is not always rational, but it is powerful, and it shapes how people relate to and trust those around them. the person who has managed to remain visibly above the fray, who appears always to be engaged with the larger picture rather than the unpleasant details, benefits from a kind of elevated status that the person who gets their hands dirty cannot achieve. Greene is careful to note the risks of this approach. the most significant risk is that the scapegoat or intermediary eventually realizes what role they have been playing and decides to expose it. this is a very real danger, and it means that the selection and management of those who carry out unpleasant work on your behalf is itself a delicate and important task. you need people who are sufficiently loyal, sufficiently dependent on you, or sufficiently self interested in the current arrangement that they will not turn on you when the consequences arrive. the failure to manage this dimension carefully has undone many people who otherwise executed this law with skill. another risk is that the pattern becomes obvious over time. if the same person is consistently associated with good news and consistently distanced from bad news, a perceptive observer will eventually notice the pattern and draw the correct conclusion. this awareness does not necessarily produce an immediate problem, but it changes the way people relate to you. they understand that you are the kind of person who uses others as buffers, and that understanding makes them more cautious about allowing themselves to be used in the same way. managing this perception requires enough variation and enough genuine visibility in both good and bad moments to prevent the pattern from becoming too obvious. the law also has an ethical dimension that Greene acknowledges without resolving. using people as scapegoats and cat's paws is, on a straightforward moral reading, a form of exploitation. it uses other people as means to your ends without their full knowledge or consent. Greene does not pretend this is virtuous. his argument is not that it is good but that it is real, that it happens, and that understanding how it works is essential whether you intend to use it or to protect yourself from it. the person who is naive about this dynamic is the person who ends up as the scapegoat rather than the one who placed them in that role. in conclusion, law twenty six teaches that the management of reputation is one of the most important ongoing tasks of anyone who seeks to maintain power over time. the world is full of dirty work that must be done, but associating yourself with that work directly is a cost that compounds over time. by developing the skill of keeping your hands clean while still accomplishing what needs to be accomplished, you preserve the most valuable asset you have, your reputation, and ensure that it continues to work in your favor long after the specific actions of any particular moment have been forgotten.
