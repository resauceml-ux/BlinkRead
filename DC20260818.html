the fifteenth and final chapter of think and grow rich addresses what Hill considers the most fundamental obstacle to the application of all the principles he has described, the six basic fears that undermine desire, erode faith, paralyze decision making, and make persistence all but impossible for those who have not learned to recognize and counteract them. these six fears, Hill argues from his research, account for the majority of the gap between what most people are genuinely capable of achieving and what they actually do achieve over the course of their lives. they are not exotic pathologies but universal human tendencies that require deliberate, conscious effort to overcome. the six fears Hill identifies are the fear of poverty, the fear of criticism, the fear of ill health, the fear of loss of love, the fear of old age, and the fear of death. of these, Hill identifies the fear of poverty as the most destructive and the most commonly encountered obstacle to financial achievement specifically, because it strikes directly at the motivational foundations that all the other principles require to function. a mind dominated by the fear of poverty cannot generate the burning desire that Hill presented in the second chapter as the essential starting point of all achievement. it cannot maintain the quality of faith that the third chapter described as essential. it cannot make the prompt, definite decisions that the eighth chapter identified as characteristic of successful people. fear of poverty, operating at the level of the dominant thought that shapes all other thinking, effectively neutralizes every positive principle before it can produce meaningful results. Hill describes the fear of criticism as the second most destructive fear specifically because of its effect on the willingness to express original ideas, to take unconventional actions, and to pursue goals that others might dismiss or ridicule. the person who is dominated by the fear of criticism will constantly edit their behavior, their ambitions, and their expressed opinions to conform to whatever seems most likely to avoid disapproval from the people around them, regardless of whether those people have achieved anything worth emulating. this constant self editing in the service of anticipated approval destroys the very quality of originality and definiteness that genuine achievement requires. the chapter provides a detailed self analysis questionnaire that Hill presents as one of the most valuable practical exercises in the entire book, a set of questions designed to help the reader honestly assess whether they are allowing any of the six fears to operate in their thinking and behavior in ways they may not have fully recognized. the questions are direct and often uncomfortable, touching on patterns of behavior and thought that most people prefer not to examine too honestly. Hill argues that this discomfort is precisely the point, that genuine self knowledge of the specific ways in which fear is limiting ones effectiveness is the necessary first step toward doing anything about it. Hill also introduces in this chapter the concept of what he calls the devil of indecision, describing procrastination as the most common and most practically destructive form that fear takes in daily life. the person who indefinitely postpones important decisions and actions is almost invariably doing so not from a carefully reasoned assessment that waiting is strategically superior to acting but from the unconscious, fear driven avoidance of the discomfort and the risk that decisive action inevitably involves. recognizing procrastination as a manifestation of fear rather than simply as a character flaw or a time management problem is itself a significant step toward addressing it at the level where it actually originates. the chapter closes with a reflection on what Hill presents as the master antidote to all six fears, the development of such complete definiteness of purpose, such burning desire, and such genuine faith in ones ability to achieve the desired outcome that the fears, however real their initial pull, are simply unable to find adequate purchase in a mind that is fully occupied with positive, directed, emotionally charged intention. this is not the suppression of fear but its displacement, the crowding out of negative, paralyzing thoughts by positive, activating ones applied with sufficient consistency and sufficient emotional intensity to gradually transform the dominant tone of the mind from fearful and hesitant to confident, directed, and persistent. it is with this prescription, simple to state and genuinely demanding to practice, that Hill concludes his life work.
