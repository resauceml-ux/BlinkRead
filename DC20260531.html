Law thirty three of the forty eight laws of power is about the art of finding what makes each person tick, and more specifically, what makes each person vulnerable to influence. Robert Greene uses the term thumbscrew to refer to the specific weakness, insecurity, vanity, or desire that gives you leverage over another person. everyone has one, and the person who can identify it has a tool of extraordinary power, the ability to move others precisely and reliably in the direction they choose, not by force but by speaking directly to the thing the other person values or fears most. Greene begins with a fundamental observation about human nature. people present the world with a public face, a managed version of themselves designed to project strength, competence, and indifference to the opinions of others. but beneath this public face lies a very different reality. everyone has needs that they feel are not fully met. everyone has insecurities they work hard to conceal. everyone has desires that are so important to them that satisfying those desires or threatening them with frustration would be enough to move them to action they might not otherwise take. the public face is a performance. the thumbscrew is the reality beneath the performance. the first technique Greene recommends for finding the thumbscrew is the use of casual, apparently aimless conversation. in formal and structured settings, people are on guard. they present the managed version of themselves and they do not reveal what truly matters to them. but in relaxed, informal settings, in conversations that do not seem to have a specific purpose, people talk more freely. they reveal what they are proud of, what they feel has not been properly appreciated, what they resent, and what they most want. these revelations are often oblique, embedded in apparently casual comments or stories, but to the alert observer they are signals pointing directly to the underlying need or insecurity that defines them. the second technique is the observation of emotional reactions. people reveal their thumbscrews most clearly in the moments when their emotional response is disproportionate to the situation. when someone becomes unusually upset by a mild criticism, that sensitivity is itself information. it tells you that the criticism has touched something they feel is important and fragile. when someone responds with unusual enthusiasm to a form of praise, that enthusiasm tells you something about what they feel they have not received enough of. these emotional reactions, precisely because they are not fully controlled, are the most reliable windows into what truly drives a person. Greene identifies several common types of thumbscrews. the need for validation and recognition is perhaps the most universal. most people feel that their true worth has not been fully appreciated, that the contributions they have made have not been properly acknowledged, and that if only others would see them as they truly are, their situation would be very different. this need for recognition is so powerful that it can override rational calculations about self interest. the person who provides recognition, who makes another person feel seen, valued, and appreciated in exactly the way they most want to be, gains a level of influence over them that is remarkable in its completeness. vanity is another common thumbscrew. the person whose self image is organized around a particular quality, whether it is their appearance, their intelligence, their taste, or their social status, is vulnerable to anything that threatens that quality and powerfully drawn to anything that validates it. flattery that touches the specific vanity of a particular person is far more effective than general praise, because it speaks directly to the thing they care most about rather than offering something more diffuse and less targeted. fear is perhaps the most powerful of all thumbscrews. the person who has identified what someone fears most has a lever that can move them in almost any direction. the most effective fears are not physical but social and psychological. the fear of being exposed as inadequate, the fear of losing status or respect, the fear of being left out or left behind, these are the fears that drive the most powerful behaviors. and because people work so hard to conceal their fears, the person who has discovered one has a piece of information that the other person desperately wishes to protect. Greene also discusses the thumbscrew of unfinished business. many people are driven by something from their past, a slight that was never answered, a failure that was never reversed, an ambition that was never fully realized. these unresolved matters become organizing principles of their behavior. they shape everything they do without the person always being fully aware of the influence. when you identify this kind of thumbscrew, you understand not just what a person wants in the immediate situation but what has been driving them across the whole arc of their life. the ethical dimension of this law is one that Greene acknowledges directly. the knowledge of a person's weakness is powerful precisely because it allows you to influence them in ways that bypass their rational defenses. this knowledge can be used to help, to motivate, to build relationships of genuine mutual benefit. it can also be used to exploit, to manipulate, and to harm. Greene is not unaware of this, and he notes that the use of this knowledge carries responsibilities that those who apply this law without reflection often overlook. the person who exploits weakness creates a specific kind of enemy if the exploitation is ever discovered, one who feels not just defeated but violated. in conclusion, law thirty three teaches that every person has a specific point of vulnerability that, once identified, gives you a form of leverage that general intelligence or general charm can never provide. by developing the observation skills and the conversational discipline to identify these thumbscrews, you gain the ability to influence the specific people in your environment with a precision and reliability that transforms your capacity to achieve what you want. the art of finding the thumbscrew is ultimately the art of understanding people more deeply than they understand themselves, and that understanding is one of the most powerful forms of knowledge available.
