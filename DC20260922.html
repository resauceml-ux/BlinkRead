having introduced the central concept of the one thing and the domino effect metaphor that illustrates its power, the authors turn to explaining the research foundation underlying their approach before beginning their systematic examination of the widely held beliefs they consider obstacles to genuine focus and extraordinary achievement. the chapter titled success leaves clues describes the authors extensive process of studying and interviewing individuals who had achieved extraordinary results across a wide range of different fields and industries, looking specifically for common patterns and common principles that appeared consistently across these otherwise very different success stories, regardless of the specific domain in which that success had been achieved. this research process, the authors explain, revealed a consistent pattern that cut across the apparent diversity of the successful individuals studied, namely that in virtually every case, the extraordinary results these individuals achieved could be traced back to an unusually narrow, concentrated focus on a specific priority or small set of priorities, rather than to the kind of broad, evenly distributed effort across many simultaneous goals that conventional productivity advice typically recommends. this consistent pattern, observed across many different fields and many different individual circumstances, provided the empirical foundation for the specific claims and recommendations that the remainder of the book develops in greater detail. having established this foundation, the authors turn to the first of what they call the six lies, widely accepted beliefs about success and productivity that the authors argue actively undermine the kind of focused achievement their research identified as characteristic of genuinely extraordinary results. the first of these lies is the belief that everything matters equally, the assumption, often unstated but pervasively influential in how most people approach their daily task lists and their broader life priorities, that all the various demands and opportunities competing for attention deserve roughly comparable time and effort. the authors challenge this assumption directly by invoking the Pareto Principle, the well known economic observation, originally developed by the italian economist Vilfredo Pareto, that roughly eighty percent of results in many systems can be traced back to roughly twenty percent of the causes or inputs. applied to personal and professional productivity, this principle suggests that the great majority of any given persons meaningful accomplishments and progress toward significant goals can typically be attributed to a relatively small subset of their total activities, while the remaining majority of activities, however legitimate or even necessary they might be in some narrower sense, contribute comparatively little to genuinely significant outcomes. the authors take this principle further than its conventional application, recommending that readers apply it recursively, repeatedly asking which specific portion of the remaining twenty percent matters most, narrowing progressively through successive rounds of this analysis until arriving eventually at a single most important task or priority, the one thing, that deserves primary focus and attention above all the competing alternatives that might otherwise claim a share of limited daily time and energy. the chapter closes by examining the practical consequences of operating according to the everything matters equally assumption, describing how people who fail to apply this kind of ruthless prioritization tend to spread their available effort and attention so thinly across the full range of tasks and priorities competing for their time that few if any of those activities receive the concentrated, sustained focus that genuinely significant achievement actually requires. the result, the authors argue, is a pattern of busy, effortful activity that nonetheless fails to produce the kind of extraordinary results that a more narrowly focused approach, guided consistently by the focusing question introduced in the opening chapters, would make considerably more achievable. this recursive application of the Pareto Principle, pushed to its logical extreme, represents one of the books most practically important contributions, offering readers a concrete, repeatable method for cutting through the overwhelming complexity of modern professional and personal life to arrive at genuine clarity about what actually deserves primary attention and effort at any given moment, rather than remaining perpetually scattered across the many competing demands that an unexamined approach to daily priority setting would otherwise allow to claim an equal share of limited time and energy. readers who begin applying this recursive narrowing process to their own task lists and priority setting often report a genuine sense of relief alongside the initial discomfort of eliminating so many seemingly legitimate demands, discovering that the resulting clarity about what truly matters most makes daily decision making considerably less exhausting than the alternative of attempting to give equal weight and equal attention to an ever expanding list of competing priorities.
