Law thirty four of the forty eight laws of power is about the fundamental relationship between self presentation and how others treat you. Robert Greene argues that the world takes you at your own estimation. if you present yourself as someone of limited importance, someone who expects little, someone who accepts whatever is offered rather than demanding what they deserve, the world will respond accordingly. it will give you exactly what you appear to expect, which is very little. but if you present yourself as someone of genuine consequence, someone who moves through the world with an expectation of respect and quality, someone who does not accept less than their worth, the world will adjust its response to match that presentation. the underlying principle is simple but transformative. act like a king to be treated like one. the central psychological insight of this law is that other people do not have independent, fully formed assessments of who you are and what you deserve. at least not initially. when they encounter you, they make rapid assessments based on the signals you send. the way you carry yourself, the confidence with which you speak, the quality of your expectations, the ease or anxiety with which you navigate social situations, all of these signals tell the people around you something about how to treat you. and crucially, these signals are under your control. they are not determined by your actual status or accomplishments. they are determined by how you choose to present yourself. Greene opens by contrasting two types of behavior in similar situations. the person who enters a room and immediately makes themselves small, who speaks tentatively, who is quick to apologize for their presence, who frames every request as a potential imposition, generates a very specific response. others treat them as someone who is of limited importance, because that is exactly the impression they have communicated. the person who enters the same room with ease, who moves deliberately and without hurry, who speaks with a quiet confidence that suggests they are used to being heard and respected, generates a completely different response. they are treated as someone of consequence, not because anything about their objective circumstances is different, but because of how they have chosen to hold themselves. the practical application of this law involves several specific behaviors. the first is the management of physical presence. how you occupy space, how you move, how fast or slow your gestures are, all of these communicate something about your relationship with your own status. people who are insecure about their status tend to make themselves physically smaller, to move quickly and nervously, to avoid taking up space. people who have internalized the belief that they belong where they are tend to move more slowly, to occupy their space with ease, and to project a physical calm that communicates comfort and authority. the second behavior is the management of speech. how you talk is as important as what you say. the person who speaks quickly and apologetically, who qualifies every statement, who asks permission before making points, communicates insecurity and the expectation that their words will not carry weight. the person who speaks at a deliberate pace, who pauses before responding rather than rushing to fill silences, who states their views clearly and without excessive qualification, communicates the expectation of being taken seriously. and that expectation tends to be fulfilled. the third behavior is the management of requests and expectations. people who undervalue themselves frame their needs as potential impositions. they apologize for asking for things. they accept inferior treatment without complaint. they take what is offered rather than asking for what they want. all of these behaviors communicate a self estimation that is below what the person actually deserves, and the response of others will reflect that communication. by contrast, the person who asks for what they want directly, who does not apologize for their needs, who expects quality and expresses that expectation without aggression or anxiety, typically receives better treatment simply because they have made clear what they consider acceptable. Greene draws on historical examples of people who rose to positions of great influence from relatively modest beginnings, not because of inherited status or exceptional talent, but because they presented themselves with such consistent confidence and such clear expectation of respect that others gradually adjusted their behavior to match. the presentation preceded the reality. the royal bearing came before the royal status, and the bearing was largely responsible for the status eventually arriving. there is also a self reinforcing element to this law. the person who begins to act as though they deserve more will, over time, actually begin to believe it more deeply. the external performance shapes the internal reality. as others respond to the projected confidence with increased respect and better treatment, the projected confidence becomes less of a performance and more of an accurate reflection of how the person actually experiences themselves. the acting and the being begin to converge, and eventually the distinction disappears. Greene also notes the important distinction between this law and arrogance. arrogance is not the same as the quiet confidence that this law advocates. arrogance is characterized by a visible contempt for others, by the explicit assertion of superiority, by a quality that says I am better than you. this generates resentment. the kingly bearing that Greene recommends is different. it is characterized by ease rather than contempt, by self possession rather than aggression, by the simple projection of someone who is comfortable in their own worth without needing to diminish the worth of others to establish it. this is the kind of bearing that commands respect rather than triggering hostility. in conclusion, law thirty four teaches that your self presentation is one of the most powerful tools available to you in any social situation. by choosing to present yourself as someone of genuine consequence, by projecting the ease and confidence of someone who moves through the world with an expectation of respect, you shape the response you receive from others in ways that can dramatically change the trajectory of your life and career. the world does not automatically recognize your value. but it will respond to your projection of it. act like a king, and the world will eventually treat you as one.
