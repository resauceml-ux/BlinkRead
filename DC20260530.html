Law thirty two of the forty eight laws of power is about the tremendous power of fantasy. Robert Greene argues that the world is full of people who are dissatisfied with the reality they inhabit. their lives feel constrained, ordinary, or disconnected from what they truly want. they yearn for transformation, for escape, for a version of their situation that is more exciting, more meaningful, or simply better in ways they find difficult to articulate but feel very strongly. this yearning is the gap between reality and desire, and Greene teaches that anyone who can speak convincingly to the desire side of that gap, who can paint a picture of the transformation or escape that people are longing for, will attract a devotion and a willingness to follow that no amount of honest, realistic assessment of the situation could ever generate. the core insight of this law is that people do not primarily make decisions based on rational analysis. they make them based on how things feel, on the stories they are telling themselves, and on their emotional responses to possibility and limitation. the person who offers a clear, sober, realistic account of a situation may be entirely correct, but they are speaking to the analytical part of the mind rather than to the emotional and imaginative parts that actually drive behavior. the person who offers a vision of transformation, even if that vision is vague and its promised timeline indefinite, speaks to a much more fundamental level of human motivation. and at that level, the response is not simply agreement. it is hope, and hope is one of the most powerful forces in human psychology. Greene opens with examples from the history of charlatans and visionaries to illustrate this principle. throughout history, figures who promised miraculous cures, secret knowledge, alchemical transformation of base metals into gold, or access to esoteric truths that could change the quality of a person's life, attracted followers and resources with remarkable ease. in many cases, these figures offered no real evidence that their promises were achievable. what they offered was the possibility, the image, the feeling that the thing might be real. and that feeling was enough. people gave money, loyalty, time, and devotion to figures who gave them nothing material in return, because what they received in exchange, the experience of hope and the image of a better reality, was worth more to them than the practical cost of their investment. Greene is careful to note that the most successful practitioners of this principle are not necessarily cynics who are simply exploiting naive people. the most compelling fantasies are those that the seller believes in themselves. when a visionary genuinely sees a possibility that others have not yet recognized and communicates that vision with authentic conviction, they are not simply manipulating their audience. they are sharing a perception that may or may not prove accurate but that is real to them. the difference between the genuine visionary and the pure charlatan may be less in the nature of what they offer than in the nature of their relationship to the truth of their own promise. the practical application of this principle involves several specific techniques. the first is the creation of a vivid and compelling image of the transformed state that you are promising. this image must be specific enough to feel real and achievable but vague enough to allow each person who hears it to fill in their own specific version of what the transformation would mean for them personally. the image that works for everyone is the one that is rich enough to attract the imagination but not so specific that it excludes the personal interpretation of each listener. the second technique is the use of contrast. the fantasy is most powerful when it is presented against the backdrop of a current reality that is explicitly acknowledged as unsatisfying or limiting. by naming the pain or the constraint that people are already experiencing, you establish that you understand their situation. by then offering the vision of what could be different, you give them hope that is grounded in the recognition of their actual circumstances rather than floating free of any connection to reality. this grounding makes the fantasy feel more credible without actually making it more specific or more rigorously defined. Greene also discusses the timing of this law. the power of fantasy is greatest when the audience is at their most dissatisfied, when the gap between their current reality and their desired state is most painfully felt. in periods of widespread uncertainty, social disruption, or economic difficulty, the person who offers a vision of a better possible world has an audience that is primed to receive it. the same message that would seem unnecessary in times of comfort and contentment becomes transformative in times of difficulty and fear. the warning Greene attaches to this law is serious. building your influence on the foundation of a fantasy carries a specific and serious risk. when the fantasy fails to materialize, and eventually it usually does, or at least reality falls significantly short of the vision that was promised, the response of those who believed is not mild disappointment. it is a betrayal response, proportional to the depth of the original hope. the more intensely someone believed in the fantasy, the more devastatingly they feel the gap when the fantasy fails to deliver. and the person who sold them the fantasy becomes the target of that devastation. managing this risk requires either delivering on enough of the promise to maintain credibility or very carefully managing the expectations and the timeline so that disillusionment is always just one more step away. in conclusion, law thirty two teaches that the gap between reality and desire is one of the most potent forces in human psychology, and that the person who can speak convincingly to the desire side of that gap will attract a level of devotion and willingness to follow that straightforward, realistic communication can never achieve. by offering people an image of the transformed reality they long for, you give them something they value more highly than almost anything else, the experience of genuine hope. and hope, once given, creates a bond of extraordinary power.
