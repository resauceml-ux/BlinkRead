Law thirty six of the forty eight laws of power is about the art of strategic indifference. Robert Greene teaches that the moment you visibly desire something you cannot have, you hand power to those who possess it or control access to it. your desire elevates the value of the thing in their eyes and in the eyes of everyone watching. it gives them leverage over you. it makes you appear needy, which is one of the most damaging impressions in any competitive environment. the alternative is a disciplined indifference, a visible disdain for the thing you cannot obtain, that communicates self sufficiency, reduces the thing's apparent value, and preserves your dignity and your strategic position. the psychological principle at the core of this law is that attention amplifies value. whatever you pursue visibly becomes more desirable in the perception of others, not because the thing itself has changed but because your pursuit signals that it is worth pursuing. this dynamic is particularly damaging when the thing you are pursuing is out of reach. the visible pursuit of the unattainable combines the signal of desire with the signal of failure, and the combination is deeply unflattering. it marks you as someone who wants what they cannot have, and this marking follows you and shapes how others relate to you. Greene opens with the observation that in any competitive environment, being denied something that you obviously wanted is a significant event. it has consequences not just in terms of what you did or did not obtain but in terms of how others read your response to the denial. the person who reacts to denial with visible frustration, with increased effort to obtain the denied thing, or with anger toward those who withheld it, communicates that the denial hurt them. and a person who can be hurt in this way is a person who has handed their opponents a reliable source of pain to exploit in the future. the disdainful response, in contrast, communicates something very different. by appearing genuinely indifferent to the denied thing, by treating it as something that never really mattered much or that is beneath your attention, you deprive your opponents of the satisfaction of having affected you. you also retroactively reduce the significance of the thing itself. if it were truly valuable and desirable, why would someone of your quality be indifferent to it. the disdain, if convincingly projected, creates a narrative in which you are above the denial rather than wounded by it. Greene also discusses the specific dynamic that occurs when you disdain something others expect you to want. the expectation of desire is itself part of the power that others hold over you. they know you want the thing. they know they have it. and they enjoy, consciously or not, the leverage this creates. by demonstrating that you do not care, you disrupt their enjoyment and undermine the leverage. you change the dynamic from one where they have something over you to one where they are left holding something that apparently has no effect on you. this shift, while it may not get you what you wanted, restores your dignity and your independent position. the law also has a broader application to the management of frustration and disappointment in general. much of what happens in competitive and social environments involves things not going as you hoped. plans fail. people disappoint. opportunities close before you can reach them. rivals get what you wanted. the person who allows these setbacks to produce visible distress gives others a detailed map of their vulnerabilities. the person who responds with apparent equanimity, who moves on without visible regret, who seems not to be diminished by what has happened, presents a much more formidable and less assailable face to the world. Greene notes that this law is not about genuine indifference. it is about the strategic projection of indifference. he is not suggesting that you should actually stop caring about things, that desire itself is a weakness to be eliminated. desire is the energy that drives action and ambition. the issue is what you reveal about your desires, when, and to whom. keeping your desires close, not broadcasting them to people who might use the knowledge against you, and projecting a quality of self sufficiency that makes it appear that you could do without most things, is the practical application of the principle. there is also a social dimension to this law. the person who appears not to need things is paradoxically often the one who attracts them. this is the psychology of scarcity operating in the social domain. the person who seems indifferent to approval tends to receive more of it. the person who seems not to need a particular relationship or opportunity tends to find it more readily available. this is because indifference communicates a self sufficiency that others find attractive and credible. it signals that your value does not depend on external validation, and that signal is itself a form of value. Greene draws on the concept of sour grapes from the famous fable to illustrate the negative version of this principle. in the fable, the fox who cannot reach the grapes declares them sour, convincing himself he did not want them anyway. this is often read as a form of self deception. but Greene argues that even this self deception, when deployed strategically and convincingly, serves the fox better than continued visible frustration would. the fox who keeps jumping for the grapes, failing, and expressing distress is in a worse position than the fox who walks away with apparent disdain. the first fox reveals both his desire and his limitation. the second, whatever he actually feels, projects self sufficiency. in conclusion, law thirty six teaches that how you respond to what you cannot have is a choice that has significant power consequences. by projecting disdain rather than desire, by appearing indifferent rather than frustrated, you preserve your strategic position, deprive opponents of leverage, and maintain the quality of self sufficiency that is one of the most powerful impressions a person can create. the world cannot wound those who appear not to need what it withholds, and this appearance, sustained with discipline and conviction, becomes a form of genuine protection.
