the fourth and fifth of the six lies addressed in these chapters concern willpower and the popular concept of a balanced life, two more widely accepted beliefs that Keller and Papasan argue require significant revision if readers are to successfully apply the focused, one thing approach that the book is ultimately recommending. the chapter on willpower draws on psychological research examining what researchers have termed ego depletion, the finding that willpower, rather than functioning as an unlimited resource equally available at any moment a person chooses to deploy it, actually operates more like a finite reserve that depletes through use over the course of an ordinary day, much as physical muscular strength depletes through repeated exertion before eventually being replenished through rest. this research directly challenges the common assumption, embedded in the phrase the authors use as their chapter title, that willpower is always on will call, ready to be summoned whenever a person genuinely decides to exercise it regardless of what demands have already been placed on it earlier in the day. the practical implication the authors draw from this research is significant for how readers should think about scheduling and sequencing their most important work. if willpower genuinely depletes through use, then tasks that require significant willpower to complete, particularly the kind of demanding, high priority one thing activities the book is centrally concerned with, should ideally be scheduled during periods when willpower reserves remain fresh and largely undepleted, typically earlier in the day before the accumulated decisions and demands of ordinary daily life have consumed a significant portion of the available reserve. deferring the most important, willpower demanding work until later in the day, after numerous smaller decisions and demands have already depleted much of the available reserve, sets up a person for reduced effectiveness precisely when their most consequential work requires their fullest capacity. the chapter addressing balance takes on one of the most pervasive and most emotionally resonant beliefs about how a successful and satisfying life should be structured, the widely held aspiration toward achieving genuine balance across the various important domains of life, work, family, health, and personal fulfillment. the authors challenge this aspiration directly, arguing that balance, understood as a stable, static equilibrium in which roughly equal time and attention are devoted to every important area of life simultaneously, is not merely difficult to achieve but is actually a fundamentally mistaken and ultimately unattainable goal, one that sets people up for a perpetual sense of falling short regardless of how much genuine effort they invest in pursuing it. the authors offer an alternative image to replace the static balance metaphor, comparing the actual lived experience of managing competing life priorities to a tightrope walker, who is never genuinely still or perfectly balanced at any single moment but is instead engaged in a continuous, dynamic process of small adjustments and counterbalancing movements that, taken together over time, allow forward progress without falling off the rope in either direction. this reframing suggests that what people actually experience and what actually works, when it works, is not stable equilibrium but continuous, dynamic counterbalancing, a pattern the authors argue is both more accurate as a description of how successful people actually manage competing priorities and more psychologically sustainable than the impossible pursuit of static, simultaneous balance across every domain. the practical recommendation flowing from this reframing is to accept, consciously and deliberately, that pursuing a significant one thing priority will often require extended periods of genuine imbalance, in which that priority receives disproportionate time and attention at the expense of other important but temporarily secondary domains, while committing to conscious, deliberate counterbalancing over time to ensure that no domain is permanently and catastrophically neglected in service of the current primary focus. these two chapters, addressing willpower and balance respectively, complete an important stage in the books argument, preparing readers to approach the final and perhaps most psychologically significant of the six lies, the deeply ingrained tendency to think small rather than genuinely embracing the scale of ambition that extraordinary results actually require, a theme the next chapter takes up directly. readers who internalize both of these reframed principles, that willpower must be strategically deployed rather than assumed to be constantly available, and that balance is better understood as ongoing counterbalancing than as a static, achievable state, find themselves considerably better equipped to sustain focused pursuit of significant goals without the recurring discouragement that a more conventional, unexamined approach to these two concepts tends to produce.
