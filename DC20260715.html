the first of the seven habits addresses what Covey considers the most foundational principle of personal effectiveness, the principle of proactivity. while the word proactive has become so commonly used in popular culture that its meaning has become somewhat diluted, Covey uses it in a very specific and philosophically rich sense that goes considerably deeper than simply taking initiative or planning ahead. for Covey, being proactive means taking fundamental responsibility for your own life, recognizing that your behavior is a function of your decisions rather than your conditions, and understanding that you have the freedom to choose your response to whatever circumstances you encounter. Covey grounds this principle in a remarkable piece of human experience shared by the psychiatrist and holocaust survivor Viktor Frankl. imprisoned in nazi concentration camps under conditions of almost unimaginable deprivation and cruelty, Frankl discovered what he later described as the last of the human freedoms, the freedom to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances. even in a situation where virtually every external freedom had been stripped away, Frankl found that his captors could not take from him the freedom to decide how he would respond inwardly to what was happening to him. this insight, that between any stimulus and any response there is a space, and that within that space lies the human freedom and power to choose our response, becomes for Covey the philosophical bedrock of the first habit. reactive people, in Covey analysis, behave as though that space does not exist. they allow the stimuli they encounter, whether difficult circumstances, other people behavior, or their own emotional states, to pass directly into automatic behavioral responses without the intervention of any conscious choice. their language reflects this, I cannot do it, he makes me so angry, I have to do this, if only things were different. each of these formulations attributes the source of behavior to forces outside the self, portraying the person as an effect of external causes rather than as an active agent capable of choosing their own responses. proactive people, by contrast, are aware of the space between stimulus and response and choose to exercise the freedom that space provides. their language reflects a fundamentally different relationship to their own behavior, I choose to, I prefer to, I will, I am going to. each of these formulations places the source of action within the person rather than outside them, reflecting an understanding of behavior as something chosen rather than determined by circumstances. Covey introduces two important concepts for understanding how proactivity operates in practical life. the first is the distinction between the circle of concern and the circle of influence. the circle of concern encompasses everything a person cares about and thinks about, from global events and other people behavior to historical facts that cannot be changed. within the circle of concern sits a smaller circle of influence, which contains only those things that a person can actually do something about through their own direct effort and action. proactive people concentrate their energy and attention within their circle of influence, working on the things they can actually affect rather than expending energy worrying about things outside their control. the result of this focus, Covey argues, is that the circle of influence gradually expands over time, as effective action in the areas within your control creates new competencies, new relationships, and new opportunities. reactive people do the opposite, spending their energy complaining about and focusing on the contents of their circle of concern, with the result that their circle of influence gradually contracts as their actual ability to affect outcomes diminishes through neglect and disempowerment. the chapter closes by emphasizing that the first habit is not about positive thinking or denial of reality but about taking genuine ownership of your own life and choices. acknowledging that you have made mistakes, that circumstances are difficult, or that others have treated you unfairly is entirely compatible with proactivity. what distinguishes the proactive person is not the absence of problems or injustice but the recognition that regardless of what has happened, the response going forward is always and ultimately a choice, and that choosing wisely and consistently is the foundation upon which all genuine personal effectiveness is built.
