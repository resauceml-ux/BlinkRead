the second and third of the six lies that Keller and Papasan address in these chapters concern two additional widely held but, in the authors analysis, fundamentally mistaken beliefs about productivity and success, the belief that multitasking is an effective and desirable approach to getting more done, and the belief that success requires comprehensive, broadly applied discipline across every relevant habit and area of a persons life. the chapter addressing multitasking draws on cognitive science research to challenge the popular and pervasive assumption that human beings can genuinely perform multiple complex cognitive tasks simultaneously without meaningful loss of quality or efficiency. the authors explain that what people commonly experience and describe as multitasking is not, in fact, the simultaneous processing of multiple tasks by the brain, but rather a rapid, continuous switching of attention back and forth between different tasks, none of which receives the brain full, undivided processing capacity at any given moment. this distinction between genuine simultaneous processing and rapid task switching, though it might seem merely technical, carries significant practical implications for how the authors want readers to think about their daily approach to work and productivity. the authors emphasize that this rapid task switching, however smoothly it might feel to the person experiencing it, imposes genuine costs in terms of both time and quality of output. each switch between tasks requires the brain to reorient its attention and reload the relevant context for the newly activated task, a process that consumes measurable time and that also tends to degrade the quality of thinking applied to each individual task compared to what sustained, undivided attention on that same task would have produced. the cumulative effect of this switching cost, repeated many times throughout an ordinary day filled with what feels like efficient multitasking, is considerably reduced overall productivity compared to the alternative of addressing tasks sequentially with full, undivided attention devoted to each one in turn. the chapter addressing discipline takes on the widely held belief that extraordinary success requires a comprehensively disciplined life, with rigorous habits and consistent self control applied uniformly across every relevant domain, from diet and exercise to work habits, financial management, and personal relationships. the authors challenge this assumption by reframing what genuine discipline actually requires, arguing that success depends not on broad, comprehensive discipline maintained indefinitely across every area of life, but on building just enough targeted discipline in a specific, carefully selected habit to establish it as an automatic, self sustaining pattern, at which point the ongoing effortful discipline that was required during the establishment period is no longer necessary to maintain it. this reframing leads the authors to an important distinction between discipline and selectivity, arguing that the people who achieve extraordinary results are typically not more broadly disciplined than everyone else across every area of their lives, but are instead more selective and more strategic about where they concentrate their genuinely limited reserves of discipline and willpower, focusing that limited resource specifically on the habits that matter most for their particular goals rather than attempting the practically unsustainable project of being equally disciplined about everything simultaneously. the authors illustrate this principle with the observation that once a specific habit has been established through this focused, temporary period of discipline, the behavior tends to become considerably more automatic and considerably less effortful to sustain, freeing up the discipline that was previously required for that specific habit to be redirected toward the next priority requiring focused attention, in a sequential rather than simultaneous approach to the systematic cultivation of the various habits that support a persons broader goals and priorities. together, these two chapters on multitasking and discipline prepare the ground for the remaining lies the book addresses, reinforcing a consistent theme that runs throughout this entire section, that genuine extraordinary achievement depends less on doing more or being broadly disciplined across every domain than on the disciplined, focused application of limited attention and limited willpower to the single priority that matters most at any given time. the lesson from both chapters converges on a single, unified insight that the authors return to repeatedly throughout the book, that genuinely extraordinary achievement is not primarily a function of doing more or trying harder across a broader range of simultaneous efforts, but of concentrating whatever limited attention, energy, and discipline a person genuinely has available on the single priority most capable of producing disproportionate results.
